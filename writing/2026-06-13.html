Your sexuality is your choice

May 13th, 2026

A reflection on the death of 1980's feminist philosophy and a subsequent attempt to resusitate the dead.

We live in an age of apparent ‘open discourse’ on sexuality. Sex is everywhere: PrEP ads litter the 1-train, pride month comes on time every June, colleges have classes philosophizing it. This era of increased sexual awareness implies an increased control over sexuality. We must ask ourselves how we ought to act as individuals with sexualities. The transsexual perspective has often gone underrepresented on this front. For this reason, prioritizing new epistemologies of transfeminisms is essential if we want to make transfeminine experience legible to feminist theory. That being said, working with the foundation laid by past thinkers and identifying the holes where their theories lie vacant can point to where we can begin to build such theories. For instance, the answer to the question “do you have to be a lesbian to be a feminist” can imply how to begin the frankly more philosophically delicious question “do you have to be a transbian to be a transfeminist?” for future inquiry. I argue that understanding sexuality, and thus lesbianism, under an existentialist framework—that is, understanding sexuality as something you do and create rather than something you simply and essentially are. This account considers cases of coercion and phenomenological desires, and strengthens the normative basis for critiquing the institution of heterosexuality. In this sense, we are all faced with a radically daunting yet deeply lucrative avenue of sexual expression and social transformation.

The conception of sexuality as something concrete, natural, and essential is a deeply modern invention; the ‘born this way’ model fails to account for the plasticity that queer individuals face when it comes to their sexualities. Essential sexuality, as I put it, describes discovering one’s sexuality as merely an act of scratching off any misleading desires in order to reveal the underlying core of one’s being: bi, homo, hetero, or ace. There supposedly exists some metaphysically discrete essence of sexuality dormant within each person (Is there a gay gene? A gay brain? A gay… soul?). Although this model has some appeal in terms of supplying pragmatic political protections for the rights of queer individuals—for example, by establishing queer people as an immutable social class subject to protections against discrimination—it falls short in accurately describing how sexuality appears in vivo.

Consider how many trans people experience sudden attraction to people and genders they previously had little to no interest in due to hormonal and/or social changes. In these cases, it makes less sense to conceptualize sexuality as something discovered than to picture it as something induced, or even created, by the individual. Is the trans woman who while pre-transition was in a heterosexual relationship, is now suddenly discovering that she is and has always been a dyke? Potentially, if one concedes that gender has some essential origin. However, if one is to take an existentialist (a. la. Beauvoir), or even a constructivist (a. la. Butler), approach to gender, the assumption that this woman was simply “always a dyke” yet “became woman” doesn’t seem wholly intuitive. Perhaps one is not born, but rather becomes, dyke.

“But what about our desires?”, the sexuality essentialist cries out. “You don’t mean to tell me we can simply control our desires?” And yet, there is a sense in which we can: we acquire tastes for coffee, cigarettes, and candles. Maybe we never enjoyed the smell of rosemary, yet have grown to enjoy it once a lover switched to a rosemary scented shampoo. The desire to smoke may seem entirely overwhelming, though through dedication one can become repulsed by the idea. Desires are highly situated in environment, body, emotions, prudence, morals. We are constantly weighing our decisions, and the decisions we desire are simply those we freely act out on. These desires are especially dependent on memory and experience; a bad memory can deter a desire for something, while the inverse is true of nostalgic/happy memories. Behavioral therapy can be used to alter the desires and behavioral patterns, either by substituting one desire for another or by simply identifying such desires but not acting upon them. In such cases, ‘desire’ can be understood as an outcome where our decision-making faculties weigh one option over another. We can interpret our desires through akrasia, where we come to desire what might not ultimately be in our ‘best interests’ (alcohol, weed, bad sex), or where we may unconsciously desire things we haven’t consciously reflected on (kink often is the most salient example of such; See Stryker 42-43), or desires we may want for one reason but not another (e.g. committing a crime yet not desiring the repercussions of such). These are all decisions made out of a lack of information, reflection, or experience. We are not making appeals to any ‘authentic’ and innate self, but rather exercising our radical freedom to choose what choice is best given the situation. The feeling, the experience, the pull of desire is our impulse to make a decision that is contextually coherent.

Thus, in moments of coercion, we say we “were not able to act as we desired” because to act as we desired is to act as aligned by the choices we deem appropriate to make. Rape is one such instance. Under the model of existential sexuality, we can move away from conceptions of rape as theft of proprety, an act of violence, or sex without consent. The first concept, that of rape as a theft of property, is problematic, as it fails to give the victim any say in the matter, rather making it a dispute between owner and thief, father/husband and rapist. The second, rape as an act of sexual violence, is promising as it provides a clear boundary of what is and what is not rape. However, there is an intuitive sense in which sexual practices such as BDSM, which include many different forms of violence, does not constitute rape as we would generally conceive of it. The final model, the consent model, and the model which the legal system bases its (penetration-centric) legislature on rape today, is more difficult to verify, as seen with the #MeToo movement. Perhaps we can conceptualize rape as an imposition of one’s ability to choose whether or not to have sex. In this scenario, a legal framework around rape structured by the existential sexuality model protects one’s bodily autonomy and thus one’s decisions to conduct a sexuality as they see fit.

Desire as it relates to sexuality functions identically as it does in any other instance of desire. We come to understand a set of choices made, choices we have clustered together to construct a given sexuality, one indicative of the best choice of action based off of the desiderata we are supplied. This provides a better explanation of conversion and aversion therapy than the model of essential sexuality, which falters under a certain edge cases. For instance, under essential sexuality, a man who is ‘exploring’ his sexuality with other men may in fact be convinced that he would rather be with a woman. In this sense, we would say his underlying sexuality was always heterosexual with a brief and inexplicable period of ‘error’ which would otherwise contradict his essential heterosexual state. But where does this error come from? From not knowing deep down that he was truly, undoubtedly heterosexual? For this reason we would have to encourage conversion therapy, as in this case it is a practical tool for ‘uncovering’ the underlying sexuality of the individual, for making the man more informed of his ‘true self’. This would be doing this man a favor, who would now get to rest easy knowing his ‘true’ sexuality. The same applies for aversion therapy, though rather than coercing an individual to conform to their inherent sexuality, it can be framed as a push away from an ‘incompatible’ sexuality.

Considering existential sexuality, we can reject all forms of conversion and aversion as impositions on the choice of the individual; they both are unnecessary coercions which act against the freedom to choose. Thus, in the scenario of the man ‘exploring’ his sexuality (a phrase unhelpful for its implications that there exists some sexual geography to map), we can rather say he is constructing, creating, inventing his own sexuality. This also clarifies what we mean when we discuss ‘Compulsory Heterosexuality’. It is that we are offered no other choice but to be heterosexual, and that prompted with new choices we would act another way—another instance of coercion. Marilyn Frye assumes this to be true, claiming that “Female heterosexuality is not a given in nature, but is actively and continuously constructed” (131), and so it is our decisions which construct female heterosexuality, not a reflection of an internal, essential sexuality. Thus I propose sexuality to be structured not like Freud’s universal bisexuality, as this too implies some innate bisexuality obscured by some mechanism of the mind, but rather that it is our decisions in life which construct the sexuality of the individual.

A concern may be that this model places those who aren’t heterosexual (who do not choose to be heterosexual) in a state of precarity, as they can “just choose to not be homosexual”. Firstly, though we can make the choice of whether or not to act out homosexuality or heterosexuality, we are firmly implanted in the situation which defines how we come to prioritize decisions. Oftentimes, the reason to be heterosexual presents itself as prudential and logical under a given situation (e.g. lavender marriages), though we can still claim that under different circumstances another choice would potentially be made. Even under the immense persuasion of the benefits of heterosexuality, the Gay Liberation Movement still had motivation to exist because of the persuasive power that a broadening expanse of choices, the desire to have greater freedoms, harbours. Furthermore, homosexuality, and specifically lesbianism, under this view becomes not just some trivial decision, but a political choice which interplays with its environment. The decision to be lesbian under a system of compulsory heterosexuality bears serious political weight to it; it is an embodied rejection of the status quo. Secondly, this places so-called ‘heterosexuals’ in a deeper state of precarity than ‘homosexuals’. Because of the way compulsory heterosexuality functions, we can say that given a greater amount of information a lesbian made a more informed decision to be lesbian, as she has considered (and potentially lived) the implications of both the heterosexual and lesbian life. A heterosexual person often only sees one side of the picture. Thus, the institution of heterosexuality is under deeper scrutiny for its impulse to enforce uninformed decisions.

Returning to the initial question, “do you have to be a lesbian to be a feminist”, we now understand sexuality to be a political choice, one enmeshed in the situation of the individual. But what kind of sexuality is ‘lesbian’? “Lesbians are not women” (Wittig 32) for if contributing to the heterosexual institution while playing the role of object makes one woman, then existing outside such an institution means lesbian cannot be woman. Thus, not only do you not have to be woman to be a lesbian, but you cannot be woman to be lesbian. In this sense, feminism posed as pro-woman is a misunderstanding; if feminism seeks to abolish the system of patriarchy which turns people into objects, then feminism is not the fight for liberation for womanhood, but from womanhood as defined by patriarchy. As Frye put it: “Feminists are, by definition, to some degree non-compliant with the patriarchal norms of femininity and rebellious against patriarchal institutions; so by this theory, feminists are to some degree lesbian. It would follow that those who are very feminist, uncompromisingly feminist, extremely feminist, radically feminist, must BE lesbians, flat out” (128). You may do feminism in the sense that you fight against patriarchy in one instance, but to be feminist your existence must fight against patriarchy and simultaneously exist outside it, you must necessarily be lesbian.

The choice to be lesbian is equivalent to the choice to be feminist. Existential sexuality provides a more intuitive framework than essential sexuality as it pertains to issues of sexual coercion and desires while also offering an outlook which emphasizes the freedom each individual has in crafting their own sexuality. Furthermore, sexuality understood as a political choice leaves potential for critiques of sexualities which perpetuate forces of oppression—namely, heterosexuality. This ‘puts the ball in their court’, so to speak, while providing a political foundation for lesbianism that resists potential charges from heterosexual normativists. As I am reminded of the prompt, it seems to me that there is no have-your-cake-and-it-too bisexual compromise. If you have already decided that the fight against patriarchy is a valuable one, you must decide if you are ready to act upon your beliefs. Theory is not made to be idly considered and neglected. Praxis is what will result in tangible, political change in the world.

Are you dyke enough?
